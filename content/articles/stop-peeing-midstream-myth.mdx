"Stop your pee midstream. That is your Kegel."

You have read it in magazines. Maybe a friend told you. As a urologist, I would like to retire it.

## What happens when you pee

Peeing is teamwork. 2 muscles take turns:

1. Your bladder wall squeezes to push urine out.
2. Your pelvic floor relaxes to open the exit.

When you stop midstream, you slam the exit shut while your bladder still squeezes. The 2 muscles fight each other.

## Why I would skip it as an exercise

**It may leave urine behind.** Repeated interruption can keep your bladder from emptying fully. A clinical trial record on this exact exercise notes that no guideline recommends it.<Ref n={1} />

**It trains the wrong reflex.** Your pelvic floor learns to tighten at the one moment it should let go.

**It is the wrong dose.** Guidelines ask for 8 lifts, 3 times a day, for 3 months.<Ref n={2} /> Those belong on your couch, on a schedule you choose.

## 3 better ways to find the muscle

1. **The gas trick.** Imagine holding in gas in a quiet room. Squeeze and lift that spot, gently.
2. **The breath.** Lie down. Breathe out slowly and lift. Breathe in and let it soften.
3. **The finger check.** With a clean finger just inside your vagina, squeeze. You should feel a light hug and a pull upward.

## Can I try it once, just to feel it?

Some clinicians use a single stop to help a woman locate the muscle. If you try it, do it once, then let the stream finish fully.

Keep your daily practice off the toilet.

## 3 bathroom habits your bladder will thank you for

- **Sit all the way down.** Hovering over the seat keeps your pelvic floor tense.
- **Lean forward a little,** elbows on your knees, feet flat or on a small stool.
- **Take your time.** Let the stream finish on its own, without pushing.

<Doctor title="See a doctor if">

- You struggle to start peeing, or your stream is slow or stops and starts on its own.
- Your bladder still feels full after you go.
- It burns when you pee, or you see blood in your urine.

</Doctor>
