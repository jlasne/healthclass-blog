## Effort or urge?

Among US women who leak, 37.5% have stress leaks only, 22% have urge leaks only and 31.3% have both.<Ref n={1} /> A walk can bring either one.

- **With your steps:** a few drops, no warning, more on hills and stairs. That points to effort, also called stress leaks.
- **After a sudden urge:** a strong need to go comes first, then the leak. It often hits near home. That points to urge leaks.

Not sure which one sounds like you? The [leak type quiz](/resources/tools/leak-type-quiz) asks 3 questions shaped like a short test from a 2006 study.<Ref n={3} />

## Why steps can push urine out

Each step sends a small jolt down through your body. Your pelvic floor catches it, step after step, under your bladder.

A cough asks for one hard squeeze. A walk asks for something else: a light, steady support that lasts. When your pelvic floor tires before the walk ends, the drops come late, often on the way back.

## What helps

1. **Build a steady hold.** Slow holds lying down first, then sitting, then standing. Then keep a light lift while you walk. Guidelines list at least 3 months of pelvic floor training as the first step for stress leaks.<Ref n={2} />
2. **Lift a little more on stairs and curbs.** A short squeeze just before a step down helps, like a squeeze before a sneeze.
3. **If urges lead, train your bladder.** For urge or mixed leaks, guidelines list bladder training for at least 6 weeks as a first step.<Ref n={2} /> Your doctor can help you start.
4. **Keep walking.** Walking is good for your body. Pick routes you enjoy while your hold gets stronger.

## Try it today

Stand tall. Lift your pelvic floor lightly, as if you hold in gas. Keep that light lift and walk 10 steps, breathing normally. Let go and walk 10 steps at rest. Do it 5 times.
