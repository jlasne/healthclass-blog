## What each one gives you

**Kegels on your own** cost nothing and fit anywhere. The catch: no one checks your squeeze. In a 1991 study, only 49% of women did a clean squeeze after a short spoken lesson.<Ref n={3} />

**A pelvic floor physical therapist** checks how your muscle squeezes and lets go, often with a gentle internal exam. Then you get a plan built for you, and hands-on care if your pelvic floor is tight or painful.

**A guided class** sits in between: a session each day with cues, a lesson each week, and no visit to book.

## What the research says

The NICE guideline offers supervised pelvic floor training for at least 3 months as the first step for stress or mixed leaks.<Ref n={1} />

A 2024 Cochrane review pooled 63 trials with 4,920 women.<Ref n={2} /> Here is what it found on how training is delivered:

- **Clinic or home:** supervision in a clinic may slightly improve results, but the evidence is very uncertain.
- **App or leaflet:** instruction through an app or online may slightly improve results over written instruction.
- **Clinic or app supervision:** little to no difference, with very uncertain evidence.

## When to book a therapist

Book a visit if you feel pain, a bulge or a heavy feeling. Book one too if you cannot feel your pelvic floor lift, or if 3 months of daily training changed nothing. A therapist and a class can work together: the visit checks your squeeze, the class keeps you training every day.
